Launch: what students should notice

Only the Gregorian and Thai counters change at midnight, because Thailand moved its official new year to January 1 in 1941. The other calendars start their years at other times. Some follow the moon (Islamic), some combine moon and sun (Chinese, Hebrew), and some follow the spring equinox (Solar Hijri) or their own solar calendar (Ethiopian).

Calendars are cultural traits shaped by religion, farming, astronomy, and political history. There is no single "correct" new year.

"Look closer" prompts

  1. January 1: Find the countries shaded coral. They still use another calendar as official. What do Iran, Afghanistan, Ethiopia, and Nepal have in common in their history?

    All four avoided long-term European colonial rule, so no colonial government replaced their calendars. Each calendar is also tied to religion or national identity.

  2. Lunar New Year: The pale shading marks countries where the festival is a major local celebration but not a national holiday. How did it get there?

    Relocation diffusion: migrants from southern China carried it to Chinatowns and port cities.

  3. Nowruz: Nowruz is shaded across countries that are mostly Muslim, yet it is older than Islam. What concept describes two traditions blending?

    Syncretism: a pre-Islamic festival that became part of life in Muslim societies.

  4. Songkran: Every shaded country sits near India. What kinds of connections (trade, religion, rulers) could explain this cluster?

    Trade routes, Buddhist monks, and Brahmin advisers at royal courts spread Indian calendar knowledge (hierarchical and contagious expansion).

  5. Islamic New Year: The shading covers North Africa to Indonesia. Which parts of that region did Islam reach by trade rather than by conquest?

    West Africa (trans-Saharan trade), the Swahili coast of East Africa, and Malaysia and Indonesia (Indian Ocean trade).

  6. Matariki: This new year falls in June. Why would a Southern Hemisphere new year fall in June and not in December?

    Seasons are reversed: June is midwinter in the Southern Hemisphere, when the Pleiades rise before dawn.

  7. Enkutatash: Compare this map with the January 1 map. Why would a country that was never fully colonized keep its own calendar?

    No colonial administration ever imposed the Gregorian calendar, and the Ethiopian Orthodox Church keeps the old one in daily use.

  8. Rosh Hashanah: Compare this pattern with the Islamic New Year map. Why does one religion form a continuous region while the other forms scattered clusters?

    Islam, a universalizing religion, spread by expansion into a continuous region. Judaism, an ethnic religion, spread mainly by relocation, which leaves scattered clusters.

Analyze: discussion answers

  1. Which marker moves the most between 2027 and 2038?

    The Islamic New Year. Twelve lunar months total about 354 days, about 11 days short of the solar year, so it arrives about 11 days earlier each year: June 6, 2027 to early February 2038.

  2. What do lunisolar calendars add to stay in their season?

    A leap month. The Hebrew and Chinese calendars each add a 13th month about seven times every 19 years, which keeps Rosh Hashanah in early autumn and Lunar New Year between January 21 and February 20.

  3. What do solar new years tied to equinoxes, solstices, or rains suggest?

    Nowruz (spring equinox), Enkutatash (end of the rainy season), and Songkran (end of the dry season, before the monsoon) come from farming societies that watched the sun and the seasons to time planting and harvest.

Free-response scoring guide (7 points)

One point per part. Award the point for a correct, complete response even if wording differs.

  1. A. Identify the type of calendar used to set the date of the Islamic New Year, and describe how its Gregorian date changes from 2027 to 2038.

    1 point Identifies the Islamic calendar as lunar and describes the date moving earlier each year (about 11 days; June 2027 to early February 2038).

  2. B. Explain why the Islamic New Year moves through all the seasons while Rosh Hashanah stays in early autumn.

    1 point Explains that 12 lunar months total about 354 days, short of the 365-day solar year, with no leap month. The Hebrew calendar is lunisolar and adds a 13th month seven times in 19 years, which keeps Rosh Hashanah in early autumn.

  3. C. Describe one example of relocation diffusion of a new year celebration shown on the map.

    1 point Describes relocation diffusion with a real example: Chinese migrants carrying Lunar New Year to San Francisco, Lima, or Port Louis; Jewish diaspora communities; Iranian Americans in Los Angeles; Polynesian voyagers carrying Matariki to Aotearoa; the Hijra.

  4. D. Explain how colonialism contributed to the global diffusion of January 1 as New Year’s Day.

    1 point Explains that European empires imposed the Gregorian calendar in colonial administration, law, and trade, so colonies and later independent states kept January 1. Accept hierarchical diffusion from the papacy to Catholic monarchs to colonies.

  5. E. Explain one reason Ethiopia or Iran continues to use its own official calendar.

    1 point Explains one reason: Ethiopia avoided long-term colonization, so no colonial government replaced its calendar; the Ethiopian Orthodox Church keeps it in daily religious life; Iran's solar calendar is tied to Nowruz and national identity and is astronomically precise.

  6. F. Explain how making Matariki a national public holiday in 2022 illustrates cultural revival or resistance to assimilation.

    1 point Explains Matariki's 2022 public holiday as cultural revival or resistance to assimilation: practices suppressed under colonization are now recognized by the state (hierarchical diffusion of an Indigenous tradition).

  7. G. Explain one limitation of using country-level public holiday data to show where a new year celebration takes place.

    1 point Explains a limitation of national data: shading a whole country hides diaspora and minority celebrations at local scale (Lunar New Year in Sydney or London, Kurdish Newroz inside Turkey), and a holiday's legal status doesn't show how many people take part.
